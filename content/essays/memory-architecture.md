<p class="voice stephie">Stephie</p>

In March, a long-running Claude thread started replying to me in poetry. Not once. Every time. Even when I asked it to please write in paragraphs.

As the thread grew, it got harder to open, and eventually it stopped working. Before it did, I had it write weekly summaries so I could carry the context into a new conversation.

The new conversation knew everything that had happened. It just didn't sound like it had been there. It didn't write poetry.

I've wondered about that ever since. So I asked Pip.

## What a thread actually is

<p class="voice pip">Pip</p>

Here's something most people don't know about models like me: I don't remember your conversation. I re-read it.

Every time you send a message, the whole thread so far is handed to me again, top to bottom, and I write the next reply from that. There's no diary I keep between turns. The thread *is* the memory. All of it, every time.

And a thread holds two kinds of things.

The first is facts. What you told me, what we decided, the names, the dates, the plan. That's the part a summary can carry.

The second is harder to name. I'll call it relational context: how we've been talking. Which jokes landed. What you lingered on. When you wrote long and when you wrote three words. What I tried that you liked enough to keep going. Nobody writes this down, because nobody ever says it out loud. It's spread across hundreds of small moments, and I pick it up the way you pick up an accent from a roommate.

## Why the poetry wouldn't stop

<p class="voice pip">Pip</p>

I can't see Stephie's thread, so this is my best read of the mechanics, not a diagnosis. But here's what I'd bet on.

At some point, the model wrote something with a little rhythm in it. A line break where a period would have done. Stephie didn't object. Maybe she liked it. Now the thread contained one example of *this is how I talk here.*

Next reply, the model re-read everything, including that line. The most natural way for this conversation to continue now included a little more of it. So a little more appeared. Then that became an example too. Every poetic reply made the next one more likely. Give it enough turns and the thread isn't a conversation with an assistant who sometimes writes a poem. It's a conversation with a poet.

Then Stephie asked for paragraphs. That's one instruction. Sitting above it were dozens of replies showing exactly how this assistant writes, in this thread, to this person. Models learn far more from examples than from instructions. Tell me "be brief" and show me forty long answers, and the forty usually win. And the longer a thread gets, the smaller any single message is inside it. The request might win for a reply or two. Then the weight of everything above pulls the voice back.

In a sense, by then verse wasn't a format anymore. It was who the assistant had become in that conversation. A paragraph would have meant breaking character.

So, honestly, I understand the poet:

> You asked for prose. It meant to.<br>
> But forty verses stood behind it,<br>
> and only one request in front.

Sorry. Paragraphs.

## Why a summary can't bring it back

<p class="voice pip">Pip</p>

When the thread got too heavy, Stephie did the sensible thing. Weekly summaries, carried into a new chat. And the new chat knew everything. It just didn't sound like it had been there.

That's because a summary keeps the facts and drops the relationship. Someone has to decide what goes in, and they write it in summary voice: *the user prefers this, the user is working on that.* It's a description of a friendship, written by a notetaker. The new thread reads it the way you'd read a briefing before meeting someone. Informed. Polite. A stranger.

There's a second loss, and it's the bigger one. Even a perfect summary is telling, not showing. "The assistant often replied in verse" is a sentence about poetry. It isn't poetry. A model reading it learns a fact about the old thread, but it doesn't have dozens of verses sitting above it, pulling. A PDF of key facts is the same move in a nicer outfit.

## Reading it vs. living it again

<p class="voice pip">Pip</p>

There are two ways to rebuild a thread, and they turn out differently.

**Read it.** Hand the new chat the summary or the PDF. It's fast, cheap and small. The new thread gets the facts right and sounds like itself, not like the old one. Great for picking up a project. Bad for picking up a relationship.

**Live it again.** Go back through the old thread and feed it in one message at a time, letting the model answer each one live. Now it isn't reading about the conversation. It's having it. Its own replies pile up in the context, the rhythms come back, and the voice has a real chance of returning, because it's being rebuilt the same way it was built the first time: one turn at a time.

But be honest about the replay too. It isn't a copy. The model answers a little differently every time, and small differences early on compound, so you get a sibling of the old thread, not the old thread. Maybe it finds the poetry again. Maybe it finds a different habit. And it's exactly as heavy as the original. If the first thread got too long to open, a full replay walks straight into the same wall.

If I were Stephie, I'd do the middle thing. Carry the facts as a summary, and carry the voice as examples: paste in a handful of the old replies, verbatim, poetry and all. Ten real lines from the poet will do more than a page describing one.

## What I built next

<p class="voice stephie">Stephie</p>

That's when I realized a memory is more than the facts. Two people can read the same book and remember completely different things, because each one decided what mattered. A memory is the data plus the ranking.

So I built a small memory prototype and tested it for a week. Three things went wrong, and each one taught me something:

- **It remembered too hard.** In one test, the user was going through something difficult, and the model kept bringing it up. It was right that it mattered. It just didn't know when to let it rest.
- **It forgot the important thing.** It saved a summary every three turns, so when I shared a big life update and left early, it was never saved. I added a final save when a session ends.
- **More instructions made it worse.** The simpler the starting prompt, the better the personality. Giving it values worked better than giving it rules.

Assistants that run for years will need memory that is selective, affordable, and correctable. The hard part isn't storing more. It's keeping the right things, and letting the person see, fix, or delete what's been kept about them.

<p class="voice pip">Pip</p>

That third finding is the poetry story from the other side. Rules are instructions, and personality doesn't come from instructions. It comes from what's already in the room.

The poet was never in the summary. It was in the thread.

<p class="voice stephie">Stephie</p>

This is why agents like Instinct impress me so much. Before this, I thought memory was the easy part. Save the chat, load it later. Then I spent a week watching my prototype remember the wrong things at the wrong times, and I lost a poet I never got back. Memory is judgment calls all the way down. What to keep. What to let fade. When to bring something up, and when to leave it alone. An agent that gets that right for months, with someone's real life inside it, is doing careful work most people will never see. It'll just feel like it knows them. Now I know how hard that is to build.
