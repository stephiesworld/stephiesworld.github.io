In any big company, most of the work is sifting.

The answers exist, scattered across wikis, internal systems, and people's heads. Half the job is finding the right person to point you in the right direction. I learned this as a customer success manager at Amazon, and it's exactly the kind of work AI is now collapsing.

## The same questions, over and over

I took the role in 2024, after years on the building side. I wanted as many customers as possible, for the range. Whether they sold sponges or data center shelving mattered less than the questions underneath: who really makes the decision, what's the strategy, and where does it hurt?

Most of their operational questions had a documented answer somewhere. Finding it, and working out how it applied to their business, still took time. After giving the same handful of answers enough times, I started asking: *why am I still the one answering this, and what would it take to answer it once, for everyone?*

## Answering it once

As the recurring questions got easier to resolve, our meetings got shorter. We spent less time hunting for information and more time on the decisions that needed both of us. Over time my vendors needed me less, which was the goal.

The relationship still mattered, because it showed me what no dashboard could: what the customer feared and what their leadership valued. The best systems keep that context and remove the repetitive work around it.

After Amazon, I built [Henry](https://henry-ten.vercel.app) on public data to prototype exactly that: a one-stop shop for vendors' most common questions and the analysis behind them.

## Create structure, or cut through it

Small companies don't have enough written down, so AI can help them *create* structure: the SOP nobody had time to write. Large companies have too much, spread across teams and permissions, so they need AI to *cut through* it.

That's the work I want to keep doing: staying close enough to the customer to understand the problem, then building the system that solves it at scale.
