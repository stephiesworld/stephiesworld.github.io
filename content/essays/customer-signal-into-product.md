In any big company, most of the work is sifting.

Knowledge lives in endless SOPs and wikis, and just as often in people's heads, passed peer to peer. Half the job is finding the right person to point you in the right direction. I learned this as a customer success manager at Amazon, and it's exactly the kind of work AI is now collapsing, by pulling scattered information into one place you can ask.

## The role

I took the CSM role in 2024. Until then I'd always worked on the back end: building the product, studying customers in the data, but not talking to them directly. Even as a founder, when I did every job, I mostly lived on the R&D and partnerships side.

Someone once asked me whether the role was basically customer support. It included problem-solving, but the more interesting part was recognizing which customer problems were one-offs and which revealed a system that needed to change.

I wanted as many customers as possible, for the range. Whether they sold sponges, beans, computers, or data center shelving mattered much less than the questions underneath. Who is the leadership team? How does the org work? Who actually makes the decision? (Sometimes those are three different people.) What's the growth strategy? What are the biggest pain points, and are the strengths being used?

## The sifting

The most interesting thing to learn was Amazon itself: a universe of internal systems, layered on top of and next to each other. It's a lot for anyone new, though it feels more manageable if you've been a founder. Getting another team's attention takes storytelling, which you already know how to do if you've ever fundraised.

Many customer questions had a documented answer somewhere: in public guidance, internal systems, or Vendor Central. Finding the right one and working out how it applied to a specific business still took time. Customers were navigating a huge volume of fragmented information. What they needed was a clear answer, grounded in the right source, explained in the context of their business.

Once you've given the same handful of answers enough times, you start asking: *why am I still the one answering this, and what would it take to answer it once, for everyone?*

## Answering it once

As the recurring questions got easier to resolve, our meetings got shorter and more focused. That's how I knew the systems were working. We spent less time finding information and more time on the decisions that genuinely needed the customer and me together.

I liked the people. Being a CSM is a bit like being a business therapist, and watching a customer grow is its own reward. The relationship mattered because it showed me what no dashboard could: what the customer was afraid of, what their leadership valued, and what constraints shaped the decision. The best systems keep that context and remove the repetitive work around it. The satisfying part was noticing the same question come up again and again, and making sure it never had to be asked twice. Over time my vendors needed me less, which was the goal.

After Amazon, I built [Henry](https://henry-ten.vercel.app) on public data only, with no confidential information, as a one-stop shop for vendors' most common questions. It prototypes the repeatable research and diagnostic side of the CSM role: answering common operational questions, structuring the financial analysis, and helping vendors walk into conversations better informed. Every one of those recurring questions was solvable.

## Create structure, or cut through it

Smaller companies usually have less process sprawl, but more tribal knowledge and fewer dedicated owners. AI can help them write down and standardize work that never got documented, like the SOP nobody had time to write.

Large companies have the opposite problem. The procedures exist, but they're fragmented across teams, systems, approval layers, and permissions. Small companies need AI to *create* structure. Large ones need it to *cut through* the structure they already have.

That's the work I want to keep doing: staying close enough to the customer to understand the problem, then building the system that solves it at scale.
