Every AI deployment needs a fundraiser

October 2026  ·  6 min read

When I ran Little Bun, I was the fundraiser. I raised a little venture money, but most of the fundraising happened in smaller rooms. Getting a retail buyer to give us shelf space when they had a hundred other things to put there. Getting a Michelin-star chef to spend time on a dumpling startup. Nobody owed me their attention. Every yes had to be earned with a story they could believe.

I've been thinking about that a lot as AI companies figure out who they need in the room with a customer.

The engineer is half the team

The forward deployed engineer has become the hero of the AI deployment, and for good reason. Someone has to sit with the customer's data, wire the model into their systems, and make the thing actually work. That's hard, and it's most of what makes a deployment real.

But most engineers I know aren't thinking commercially while they do it. They shouldn't have to be. They're trying to architect the product, figure out the plumbing, and get the evals to pass. That's a full-time job. Asking the same person to also read the politics of the org they're deployed into is asking for two jobs in one hire. Once in a while you find someone who can do both. Very rarely.

The other seat

So who sits next to the engineer? Not the role most companies reach for. The old version of that seat was built for software that got sold once and then needed to be kept alive: watch the usage dashboard, send the reminder, run the quarterly review. In an AI-native company, that person as-is doesn't have much to do. The software doesn't need babysitting. It needs a champion.

What the engineer needs is a partner who understands how organizations actually behave. People, power, politics. Who really decides. Who is quietly against this. Which VP needs a win before the end of the year, and which one got burned by the last AI pilot. None of that is in the data the engineer is looking at, and all of it decides whether the deployment lives.

If I had to write the job description in one line: give me a complicated customer, a bunch of imperfect signals, a powerful technology, and an outcome we're trying to reach, and I'll figure out how to move the system.

Fundraising, on two sides

The best way I can describe the job is fundraising.

On the inside, you're raising attention. Every team at the customer has a roadmap, and your project is competing with everything else they could be doing. Someone has to get people to care about this work and prioritize it when they have a hundred reasons not to. That's a pitch. You make it over and over, to different people, in their language.

On the outside, you're raising money. Someone has to pay for this. The budget holder doesn't care how elegant the retrieval pipeline is. They care what changes for their business, how soon, and what it costs them to wait. Turning the engineer's work into that story is its own skill.

A founder does both of these every day. That's why I think this seat should be hired like a founder, not like a support function. Commercial instinct, a feel for operations, and the nerve to call people and ask for things.

After the contract

The contract is not the finish line. Usage looks healthy, the meetings are polite, and it will probably renew. Underneath all of that, the organization still has to change how it works. That's the inside raise, and it's the longer one.

A good founder walks into a meeting already asking: what is this person's world? What hurts? Who else influences the decision? What would have to be true for saying yes to become obvious? Then they build the path.

After the contract, you're selling the next behavior, and every behavior has a different buyer.

Here's the usual stall, with an AI coding tool. One engineer has had it open for a few weeks. In a demo it looks like a different job. In standup it's invisible. The team lead isn't opposed. They're responsible for a sprint that still has to close, and for code review that still has to mean something if half the diffs arrive in a shape the rest of the team can't read yet.

Enthusiasm won't get you the next buyer. A smaller surface might: one repository, one kind of change, a review rule the lead can defend, until there's a week they can point to. That week is the proof. The proof is what a manager can carry into a room. The room is how the next team hears about it.

A composite of the ordinary kind. The mechanics are what matter.

One conversation gets you a pilot stuck on a single laptop. The org moves when each buyer gets their own answer to what would have to be true?

Five, then fifty, then five hundred

To reach ten thousand engineers, you need five people who can persuade fifty, who create the evidence that persuades five hundred.

A champion is already inside the system, with credibility, motivation, and access. Give them evidence, a story, resources, and an opening, and they can move it. The team lead who will defend the review rule is the champion for that step. Organizations change from the inside, one champion at a time.

The contract renewed. A year from now the dashboard can still look fine and the meetings still polite. What the year was for is slower to see: a team that ships differently, a number that moved, a story that traveled far enough to bring the next team with it. That path doesn't appear on its own. Someone makes it, one buyer at a time.

What I saw at Amazon

I spent two and a half years working with some of Amazon's biggest sellers. The days that mattered were never the ones where I followed a playbook. They were the ones where I understood a seller's business well enough to see a move that helped both sides, and then sold it on both sides. Sold it to their team, sold it to mine. The best chess moves are the ones where everybody grows.

Selling it to mine was the part nobody warns you about. Amazon is a universe of teams layered on top of each other, all of them swamped, none of them with a reason to answer you. It overwhelms a lot of new hires. It didn't overwhelm me, because I'd fundraised. You have a better shot at a reply from another Amazonian than from an investor trying to ghost you, and you get it the same way: with a story worth their time.

That's the job I'm describing. It just doesn't have a good name yet.

TL;DR

  • AI deployments need two people: the engineer who builds it, and the strategist who gets it funded and prioritized.
  • They're rarely the same person, and that's fine.
  • The old keep-the-account-happy role doesn't survive the AI-native world as it is.
  • After the contract, every next behavior has a different buyer. Win them one at a time, through champions who are already inside.
  • Hire the second seat like a founder: someone who reads org politics, tells the story, and raises attention inside and money outside.
