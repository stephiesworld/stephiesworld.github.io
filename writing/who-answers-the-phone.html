Who's going to answer the phone

October 2026  ·  7 min read

Every company has a phone number, and almost nobody likes answering it. That is the whole pitch for voice agents, and in 2026 it stopped being a demo. Investors put billions into voice AI in the first quarter alone. ElevenLabs reportedly passed half a billion dollars in annual revenue. Sierra says voice has overtaken chat as its main channel.

"Voice AI company" covers everything from a model that turns text into sound to a finished agent that negotiates truck rates. I wanted to know who is actually competing with whom, so I drew the map.

The map

Read it two ways. Up is how finished the product is. At the bottom you buy a model and build everything else yourself. At the top you buy an agent that already knows how to reschedule a dentist appointment. Right is who signs the check: developers, then any business, then one specific industry. On a laptop, hover a dot for what each company is good at and who it sells to.

The pink lines are where companies are heading. Almost all of them point at the same place: the enterprise phone line. A big company, millions of calls a year, a contact center it would like to shrink.

The three layers

Models: the ears and the mouth

Deepgram and Mistral turn speech into text. ElevenLabs and Cartesia turn text into speech. OpenAI Realtime and Gemini Live do the whole thing in one model: audio in, audio out. You pay per minute or per character, and the price keeps falling. ElevenLabs cut its agent pricing to about ten cents a minute early this year. Mistral lists real-time transcription at $0.006 a minute.

Being the best at this layer matters, but not for long. A better voice or a faster transcriber is a lead measured in months.

Builder platforms: the plumbing

Making a model talk is easy. Making it talk on a phone is hard. Someone has to handle the phone number, the caller who interrupts, the voicemail, the transfer to a human, the call that drops, and the test suite that proves last week's change didn't break any of it. That is what Vapi, Retell, Synthflow and LiveKit sell. Vapi says it has processed over a billion calls. Analysts at Sacra estimate Retell's revenue run-rate at about $60M, up more than six times in a year.

Bland is the interesting one here. It reportedly dropped about half its customers to focus on enterprise, runs on its own models, and will deploy inside a customer's own cloud or data center. It is climbing out of this layer on purpose.

Finished agents: the job, done

At the top, nobody is buying minutes. They are buying resolved calls. Sierra, Decagon, Parloa and PolyAI sell to any big company with a support line. HappyRobot, Assort Health and Hippocratic AI pick one industry and go deep: freight brokers booking trucks, clinics booking patients.

What they really sell is the stuff around the model: connections into the customer's systems, the evals that prove the agent won't promise a refund it can't give, and people who sit with the operations team until it works.

Anatomy of one call

Why does any of this need a whole industry? Because talking is fast. In conversation, people leave about a fifth of a second between turns. Anything much slower than that feels like a bad connection.

There are two ways to get faster. Make every box faster, which is what Deepgram, Cartesia and ElevenLabs compete on. Or collapse the middle into one model, which is what OpenAI and Google are betting on. The collapsed version sounds more natural and handles interruptions better. But it is harder to inspect, harder to test step by step, and you can't swap in a different voice or a cheaper transcriber. That is why many enterprise deployments still run the cascade: a bank would rather be slightly slow than unable to explain what its agent heard.

Where the money went

The money sits at the top of the map. Finished agents are worth the most because that is where the budget comes from: not the engineering budget, but the contact center line item, which at a large company can be hundreds of millions of dollars a year in people and software. ElevenLabs is the exception that proves it. Its valuation is a bet that the best-known voice company can climb into that budget too.

Is Mistral coming?

It is already here, just not as an agent yet. This year Mistral shipped Voxtral Transcribe 2 in February, then Voxtral TTS in March: a speaking model in nine languages that can clone a voice from a few seconds of audio. Mistral says listeners preferred it over ElevenLabs' fast model in most side-by-side tests. Its real-time transcription model is open-weight under Apache 2.0. Put those together with a Mistral language model and you have every box in the diagram above, and you can run all of it in your own data center.

What Mistral doesn't have is everything above the model: phone numbers, turn-taking, an agent builder, and the people who sit with an operations team for six weeks. So my bet is that it shows up through partners first, aimed at the customer Parloa (Berlin) and PolyAI (London) already serve: a European bank, insurer, telco or government office that can't send call audio to a US cloud. Its first real damage will be on price. Open weights make ElevenLabs and Cartesia defend their per-minute rates long before they threaten Sierra.

Who isn't on the map

The contact center incumbents. Genesys, NICE, Five9, Salesforce and Twilio already own the phone line, the routing and the seat licenses. NICE bought Cognigy last year to get its own agents. Their move is to bundle. The startups' move is to get to the call before it reaches them.

The labs. OpenAI and Google set the floor. Every time they ship a better real-time model, the bottom of the map gets cheaper and the top gets better margins. That is good news for anyone selling finished agents, and a problem for anyone selling a voice.

Where it's heading

  1. Everyone is walking toward the enterprise phone line. Model companies add agent builders. Platforms go upmarket. Vertical players go horizontal. In two years the map will have fewer layers and more overlap.
  2. Sounding human stops being the edge. Voice quality is converging. What's hard to copy is knowing a customer's systems and proving the agent won't say something expensive. Evals beat voices.
  3. Where it runs becomes a feature. Open weights, private clouds and on-prem deployments matter most to the buyers with the biggest call volumes: banks, insurers, telcos, governments.
  4. Pricing moves from minutes to outcomes. Minutes are a commodity. A rebooked flight or a scheduled patient is not.

The phone is the oldest interface most companies still run. The interesting fight isn't who sounds most human. It's who gets trusted with the call that matters: the refund, the reschedule, the truck that has to move tonight.
